# Research and Limitations

This page separates what is established about lucid dreaming from what dDNS is
guessing. Short version: the techniques underneath dDNS have research behind
them. The Wi-Fi cue and the router anchor do not. This is a personal
experiment.

## 1. Lucid dreaming

A lucid dream is a dream in which you are aware that you are dreaming. It has
been verified in sleep laboratories: trained lucid dreamers can signal from
inside REM sleep using pre-agreed eye movements, which show up on recordings
while other measures confirm the person is asleep. Lucid dreams vary a great
deal in clarity and length, and many are brief.

## 2. Dream incubation

Dream incubation means deliberately focusing on a theme before sleep in the hope
that it will appear in dreams. Daytime and pre-sleep experiences do influence
dream content, and studies have used pre-sleep rehearsal, and in some cases
sounds or spoken prompts during sleep onset, to steer dream themes. Influence
is partial and unreliable: an incubated theme may appear distorted, appear only
in passing, or not appear at all.

dDNS uses incubation only to make Wi-Fi more likely to come up. Wi-Fi appearing
is not the goal; it is the trigger.

## 3. MILD and prospective memory

MILD (mnemonic induction of lucid dreams) was developed by Stephen LaBerge. It
uses prospective memory, the ability to remember to do something when the right
moment arrives. Before sleep, you rehearse an intention of the form "next time I
am dreaming, I will recognize that I am dreaming," and you visualize yourself
back in a recent dream, noticing a dream sign and becoming lucid.

Reviews of lucid dream induction methods have found MILD to be among the better
supported techniques, though the evidence base is small and success rates vary
widely between people and studies. Larger studies of MILD combined with a brief
night-time awakening (WBTB) have reported that people who fell asleep quickly
after the MILD rehearsal were more likely to have lucid dreams. dDNS borrows
this lesson in a gentler form: rehearse briefly, then sleep. Do not lie awake
rehearsing.

## 4. Cues during sleep

Laboratory studies have presented sounds, lights or other signals during REM
sleep, sometimes after pre-sleep training that associates those signals with
becoming lucid, and found that this can sometimes increase lucidity or allow
communication with a lucid dreamer. These are controlled studies with
researchers monitoring sleep. dDNS 0.1.0 does **not** present anything during
sleep. The cue is internal: it relies entirely on memory.

## 5. What is untested

- No study has examined Wi-Fi, connectivity or any networking concept as a
  lucid-dream cue.
- No study has examined a router, or any similar object, as a physical anchor
  for MILD rehearsal.
- The argument in [architecture.md](architecture.md) that an invisible,
  assumed capability is a good cue is a design argument, not a finding.

Treat every night as a data point in an experiment with one participant.

## 6. What counts as lucidity

| Event | Lucid? |
| --- | --- |
| You dreamt about Wi-Fi, routers or networks. | No. |
| You dreamt you asked "Am I dreaming?" and answered "no". | No. |
| You knew, during the dream, that you were dreaming. | Yes. |
| You knew it only after waking. | No. That is recall. |
| You were lucid but couldn't do anything you planned. | Yes. |

The success condition is recognition, during the dream, that it is a dream.
Control is not required.

## 7. Limits of dream memory

Dream memories fade quickly after waking and are easily reshaped while being
recalled. Writing them down helps but also edits them. Some practical
consequences:

- Record immediately and briefly, before checking your phone.
- Record what you remember, then separately what you are unsure about.
- Do not upgrade a score later because the dream "feels" more lucid in
  retrospect.
- Do not treat anything learned in a dream as factual information about the
  world.

## 8. Sleep health boundaries

Sleep is more important than this experiment. dDNS 0.1.0:

- does not use alarms to interrupt sleep;
- does not ask you to stay awake or shorten sleep;
- does not involve alcohol, cannabis, supplements, or any medication change;
- does not touch medical devices or their alarms;
- keeps bright screens out of the bedtime routine.

**Stop**, and simply sleep normally, if any of these happen:

- it takes noticeably longer to fall asleep, or you wake more at night;
- you feel anxious about bedtime or about "succeeding";
- nightmares become more frequent or more distressing;
- you have episodes of sleep paralysis that are frightening;
- you find it harder than usual to tell, after waking, what was a dream.

If problems with sleep, nightmares or confusion persist after stopping, talk to
a doctor. People with conditions affecting sleep, mood or perception should check
with their clinician before trying lucid dreaming practices.
